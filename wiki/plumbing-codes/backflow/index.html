What is Backflow?

Curator: John Masterson | Master Plumber
Fact Checked: Active
Updated: 2026-08-16
AI Overview & Quick Facts
Backflow is the undesirable reversal of flow of water, wastewater, or other liquids, mixtures, or substances into the distributing pipes of a potable water supply system. It is categorized into two distinct hydraulic phenomena: backsiphonage and backpressure.

Core Concept & Mechanism

Understanding the mechanical principles behind backflow is vital for municipal water safety and residential plumbing integrity. Backflow occurs under two conditions:

  • Backpressure: Occurs when the pressure in a downstream, non-potable system exceeds the pressure in the supply system. This can be caused by pumps, thermal expansion in boilers, elevation heads, or pressurized systems that push contaminated water backward past a cross-connection.
  • Backsiphonage: A form of backflow caused by negative or sub-atmospheric pressure in the potable water supply. This mimics a vacuum, sucking contaminated water from fixtures or open sources back into the clean lines. Common triggers include high-velocity firefighting operations or water main breaks nearby.

Plumbing systems must be designed to isolate potable water lines from hazardous cross-connections by implementing either physical barriers (like air gaps) or mechanical assemblies (such as RPZs, double check valves, or vacuum breakers) selected specifically for the degree of hazard present (high-hazard vs. low-hazard).

Code & Technical Regulations

Both the IPC and UPC heavily regulate backflow protection devices. The selection of backflow preventer depends on whether the system is subject to backpressure or backsiphonage, and the severity of the hazard:

Parameter / Device IPC Section / Requirement UPC Section / Requirement
General Requirements IPC Section 608 (Protection of Potable Water) UPC Section 603 (Cross-Connection Control)
Atmospheric Vacuum Breaker (AVB) Min 6" above flood rim; no downstream valves (Section 608.15.4) Min 6" above flood rim; no downstream valves (Section 603.5.1)
Pressure Vacuum Breaker (PVB) Min 12" above flood rim; permits downstream valves (Section 608.15.4.1) Min 12" above flood rim; permits downstream valves (Section 603.5.2)
Reduced Pressure Zone (RPZ) Required for High Hazard backpressure & backsiphonage (Section 608.16.2) Required for High Hazard backpressure & backsiphonage (Section 603.5.6)

Journeyman & Master Exam Prep Insights

  • Backsiphonage vs. Backpressure: Expect questions on the Journeyman Plumber Exam asking to identify scenarios (e.g., "A municipal water line breaks down the street and water is drawn back..."). This is always backsiphonage.
  • Degree of Hazard: Candidates studying for the Master Plumber / Contractor Exam must memorize which devices are rated for high-hazard (toxic) installations versus low-hazard (non-toxic) installations. For example, a Double Check Valve Assembly (DCVA) is only allowed for low-hazard applications under both codes.
  • Installation Heights: Memorize the installation elevation rules: AVB must be installed a minimum of 6 inches above the flood rim of the highest fixture, while a PVB or SVB must be 12 inches above.
