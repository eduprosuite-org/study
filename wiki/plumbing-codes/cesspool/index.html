What is a Cesspool?

Curator: John Masterson | Master Plumber
Fact Checked: Active
Updated: 2026-08-16
AI Overview & Quick Facts
A cesspool is an obsolete, unsealed underground pit lined with stone, brick, or concrete rings that receives raw sewage. It allows liquid waste to leach directly into the surrounding soil while solids accumulate, posing severe risks of groundwater contamination.

Core Concept & Mechanism

Cesspools represent the precursor to modern on-site sewage facilities (OSSF). Unlike septic systems, which utilize a water-tight, two-compartment settling tank to separate solids from liquid before discharging clarified effluent to an engineered soil absorption field, a cesspool combines collection and percolation in a single, unsealed pit. Because the bottom and sides are open to the earth, raw pathogens, nitrates, and chemicals leach directly into the local water table without proper aerobic soil filtration.

Due to the extreme risk of transmitting waterborne diseases (like cholera, dysentery, and hepatitis) and polluting shallow drinking water wells, new installations of cesspools are universally prohibited across the United States. Modern health and plumbing codes mandate that existing cesspools encountered during building renovations, property sales, or system failures must be decommissioned, backfilled, and replaced by a municipal sewer connection or an approved septic system.

Code & Technical Regulations

Model plumbing codes and EPA regulations target the elimination and safe decommissioning of these vintage systems:

Regulatory Parameter IPC (International Plumbing Code) UPC (Uniform Plumbing Code)
Permit for New Systems Section 301.3: Universally prohibited. Sewage must enter an approved, engineered septic system or public sewer. Section 301.3: Banned. All new installations must connect to public sewer or code-compliant private disposal.
Mandatory Connection Based on local authority rules when a public sewer main becomes available. UPC Section 713.4: Connection to public sewer is mandatory if main is within 200 feet of the property.
Abandonment Protocol Must be pumped dry, treated, and filled with clean earth, sand, or gravel. Section 722.0: Must pump dry, remove the lid, and fill the pit with concrete, sand, or gravel.

Journeyman & Master Exam Prep Insights

  • Prohibited Status: On the Journeyman Plumber Exam, remember that cesspools are universally prohibited for new construction. If asked how wastewater from water closets must be treated, the answer will always point to an approved septic tank/drainfield or public sewer system.
  • Decommissioning Steps: Licensing questions on the Master Contractor Exam frequently cover the steps for abandoning a cesspool or septic tank: **pump out the contents, remove or collapse the cover, and fill the void completely with sand, gravel, or concrete** to eliminate cave-in hazards.
  • Public Sewer Proximity: Memorize the UPC rule: if a public sewer line is extended within **200 feet** of a building served by a cesspool, the building drain must be connected directly to the sewer and the cesspool decommissioned.
  • For municipal and private water system study resources, explore our General Free Prep Portal and state standards on the Tradesman & Inspector page.

Official Citations

  • US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA): Safe Drinking Water Act Class V Injection Well regulations (banning large-capacity cesspools).
  • International Code Council (ICC): IPC Section 301 (General Regulations).
  • International Association of Plumbing and Mechanical Officials (IAPMO): UPC Section 722.0 (Abandonment of Cesspools).
